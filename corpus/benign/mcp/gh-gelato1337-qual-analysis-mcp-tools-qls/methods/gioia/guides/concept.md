# Writing a 1st-order concept

A 1st-order concept records one thing informants say, in a form the informant would recognise as
what they said. It is the unit everything else is built from, so it must keep the meaning.

## Before you write any

Read the whole interview first. Many informants circle back, qualify, or contradict an early answer
later; a concept written from the first mention can miss that.

## One concept = one idea

- One claim, practice, experience, distinction or expectation the informant expresses.
- Keep the parts that carry the meaning: **who** (developers, customers, management, "us"), **what**,
  **direction** (welcome, worry, resist, predict), **conditions** ("in regulated fields", "for
  small teams", "within five years").
- When an informant says two things in one breath, write two concepts.
- When an informant says the same thing three times, write one concept with the clearest quote, and
  add the others only if they add something.

## Label

- English, short (3 to 8 words), close to the informant's terms and distinctions.
- Says something. "Nurses distrust the triage score at night" carries meaning; "trust in tools" does
  not. Test: could you have written this label without reading the interviews? Then it is too generic.
- Not a theory word. If the label is a term from the literature, ask whether the informant said it or
  you did.

## Description

One or two sentences in English: what informants say or do, with the who / direction / conditions.
Write it for a reader who has not read the interview: it is what the theme stage will read.

## In vivo

The informant's own word or phrase, in the original language, when they have a name for it. Leave it
empty rather than inventing one.

## Quotes and reasons

- At least one quote: the shortest span that carries the meaning, copied exactly.
- Each with a reason: one line on why this quote shows this concept ("names staffing at night as the
  reason for overriding the score"), not "relevant".
- The quote must be the informant's. Agreement with the interviewer's suggestion is weak evidence;
  say so in the reason if you use it.

## New concept or existing one?

Look at the codebook and search first (`codebook()`, `search()`).

| The idea is | Do |
|---|---|
| the same as an existing concept (same who, what, direction) | `add_evidence` to it, with a reason |
| close, but with a different nuance, condition or stance | a new concept; a `meaning` memo on both saying how they differ |
| something the codebook has no place for | a new concept; mention it in the interview's `residual` memo |

When in doubt, keep them apart. Merging later is cheap and logged; a nuance lost now is gone.

## How many

As many as the interview carries. A long expert interview can easily give 15 to 30. Do not stop at a
few headline topics, and do not code small talk or the interviewer's framing.
