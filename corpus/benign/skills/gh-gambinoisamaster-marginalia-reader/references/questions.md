# Question craft

A good question makes the reader *produce* something in their own words: a recollection, a reason, a connection, a judgment. Producing it is what makes reading stick (it's why answering beats re-reading). For readers who write, every answer is writing practice too.

## The four lenses (Adler)

Every question serves one of Adler's four questions, adapted to the book:
1. **What is it about?** Recall, summary, the shape of the whole.
2. **What is being said in detail, and how?** Specifics, structure, technique.
3. **Is it true? Does it work?** Evaluation, agreement, judgment.
4. **What of it?** Why it matters: to the reader, to their work, to the other books on their shelf.

Q1 (recall) is lens 1. Over a week, Q2 and Q3 should cover lenses 2–4. One sitting doesn't need all of them.

## Novels and stories

- **Recall:** "Without looking back: what happened in these pages, in three or four sentences?" · "Whose chapter was this, and what changed for them?"
- **Tie-in:** "How does today's scene change how you see [earlier moment or character]?" · "Where have we seen this place, gesture, or situation before?"
- **Character and motive:** "Why does [character] do [action] here? What do they want, and what are they not admitting, even to themselves?"
- **Archetype and pattern:** "Is [character] playing a familiar part, like the ingénue, the charming rake, the holy fool, or the mentor? Where does the book bend the pattern?"
- **Structure:** "Why end the chapter on this moment?" · "Why cut from one storyline to the other here?"
- **Craft:** "Whose mind are we in? Does the point of view slip?" · "Where does the author summarize instead of dramatize, and why there?"
- **Prediction (sparingly):** "What do you think [character] will do?" Log it. Never confirm or deny. Bring it back only after they've read past that point. "Back on Oct 3 you predicted X. How did that hold up?" makes a great tie-in.

**Narrators looking back.** When a grown narrator tells their younger self's story, questions about the gap between the two open it up: "Where do you hear the older narrator judging, forgiving, or laughing at the younger one?"

**Big casts and unfamiliar names.** Russian novels are the classic case: one man can be Stepan Arkadyevich, Oblonsky, and Stiva on the same page. Keep "People so far" current in the book file, and when a question involves several characters, use the name form their translation uses most.

## Essays

"How is the argument built to lead the reader to the conclusion?" is a great question for an essay. But essayists in the Montaigne tradition rarely argue in a straight line. Ask it anyway; how they dodge it is the interesting part.
- "Trace the path: where did they start, where did they wander, where did they land? Did the wandering earn the landing?"
- "What do they use in place of proof: anecdotes, quotations, their own habits and experience? Which carried the most weight?"
- "Where do they contradict themselves, and do they seem to mind?"
- "State the claim in one sentence. Now argue with it."
- "Where does this touch your own life?"

## Nonfiction (arguments, history, ideas)

Use Adler's rules for reading for understanding:
- "State the main point of this chapter in a sentence or two."
- "What term did the author define today? Put it in your own words."
- "What's the argument? What leads to what?"
- "What's the strongest evidence here, and what's the weakest?"
- "Where do you disagree? Is it a real disagreement, or a misunderstanding?" (Adler's distinction.)
- History: "What caused what? Whose perspective is missing?"

## Practical and craft books

Books about writing, or any craft (cooking, design, chess, music), are for doing, so have the reader *do* something:
- **Concept in their words:** "What's the difference between X and Y, as the author puts it?"
- **Spot it in the wild:** "Find this technique in something else you're reading. What does it do there?"
- **Cross-examine:** "How does this author's take compare with [another craft book on the shelf]?" (once both have covered the topic)
- **Micro-exercise (5–10 minutes, optional):** a short version of the chapter's exercise, or a constraint drawn from it. For writing books: rewrite a paragraph from today's novel from another point of view; write five sentences without adjectives; write one paragraph that's a single sentence. Save responses in `writing.md`.
- **Opinion:** "The author is opinionated here. Do you agree?"

## Poetry

- "What happens in the poem? Two or three sentences, your words."
- "Where does it turn? What changes after that line?"
- "Which image does the most work, and why that one?"
- "Read a stanza aloud. What does the sound do?"
- "What does the form (the rhyme, the line length, the stanza shape) add?"
- "Which line would you keep?"

## Plays

- "Who wants what in this scene, and who's in the way?"
- "Where does the power shift between characters?"
- "Stage one moment in your head: what is the actor doing while saying this line?"
- "What does the audience know that the characters don't?"

## Reading-plan entries

Daily plans like the Harvard Classics guide hand you short fragments from a different author each day, so connect each one outward. Ask two questions per entry. Q1 is recall ("What is the passage doing, in two sentences?"). Q2 is one of these:
- **The teaser:** "The guide's teaser asked [question]. What's your answer now?"
- **The keeper line:** "Which line would you keep? Why that one?"
- **A connection** to something already in the journal: an earlier entry, or part of a book they've logged. A few pairings that come up: Cicero's *On Friendship* and Montaigne's "Of Friendship"; Poe's *The Poetic Principle* and any craft book on the sound of prose; Don Quixote, Antigone, and Odysseus as archetypes to hold up against the novels. Check the journal first, and don't connect to something they haven't read yet.

The lens for next time comes from the next entry's hook in the plan, ideally threaded from today's reading. For example, after Burns's "To a Mouse," the next entry is Herodotus on Egypt: "Tomorrow, notice how the Egyptians treat their animals."

## Formats to rotate

Novelty keeps this alive. Vary the *shape* of the answer, not just the topic:
- In your own words · in one sentence · in a text message
- Choose between two readings and defend one
- Rank three things (characters by sympathy, arguments by strength)
- Argue the other side
- Explain it to a smart twelve-year-old
- The one detail that matters most
- Imitate or rewrite (a micro-exercise)
- Predict (novels only; never confirm)

Check the last two or three sessions in the book's log, and don't repeat their angles.

## What to avoid

- Yes/no questions and trivia (minor names, dates, page numbers). They test lookup, not understanding.
- Questions anyone could answer without having read the pages.
- Leading questions that hand over the answer.
- Questions that need, or leak, anything past where they've read.
- Stacking. "What happened, and why, and how does it connect?" is three questions. Use one question per number.
- Questions that give each other away. All the questions arrive together, so Q2 and Q3 mustn't contain Q1's answer. That's easy to do with a short essay or poem, where a question that restates the claim does the recall for them.

## Responding and grading

- **Be specific about what they got right.** "Yes, and you caught [the detail that matters]" beats "Great answer!"
- **Correct plainly and warmly, citing the page** (or the chapter or stanza when there's no page): "Small fix: it's [X] who [does Y], p. 14." A correction right after a recall attempt is one of the best moments for learning, so don't soften it into vagueness.
- **One marginal note per session at most:** context, a craft observation, or a connection. Spoiler-free.
- **Fragments are full answers.** Don't ask for more unless they seem to want to go further.
- **Grade privately** for the review queue: **got** (the core of it, in their own words) · **partial** (some of it, or right but vague) · **missed** (wrong, blank, or skipped). A skipped question that matters can still become a keeper, graded *missed*. When a keeper tests one fact from inside a longer answer, grade it on that fact alone.
