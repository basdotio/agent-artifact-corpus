# Cover letters

## When it matters

Survey data (Resume Genius, 625 hiring managers) says most read cover letters, mostly in under two minutes. Engineering practitioners say large companies mostly ignore them. Letters carry the most weight at small companies and startups where a founder or hiring manager reads every application, for career pivots or level jumps, for return-from-break candidates, when there's a referral, and when emailing a hiring manager directly (then the email is the letter). Tell the user which case this is; for a big-company portal with an optional letter, a short one is still worth sending, but don't spend long on it.

## Shape

- 180-300 words. Three or four short paragraphs. One page, same header as the resume.
- **Opening (2-3 sentences):** the specific match. Their problem, in their words, and the evidence that you've solved it before. Or the referral ("Dana Kim on your platform team suggested I write"). Or a concrete detail about their product, engineering blog, or talk that connects to your work. Name the role once.
- **Middle (1-2 paragraphs):** 2-3 of the JD's main needs mapped to concrete work, told with more context than a resume bullet allows: why it was hard, what you decided, what happened. One story beats three summaries. Don't restate resume bullets word for word.
- **Close (1-2 sentences):** a plain next step, optionally one real question or observation about their stack or problem. No begging, no "I look forward to the opportunity to discuss how my skills align".
- Greeting: the hiring manager's name if known; otherwise "Hello [Company] team," or "Dear Hiring Manager,". Sign-off: "Thanks," or "Best," then name.

## What hiring managers dislike

Restating the resume; "passionate and innovative"; "I am excited to apply"; "I am writing to express my interest"; templates where only the company name changed; flattery of the company with no specifics; length; claims of fit without evidence ("I'm a perfect fit"); apologizing for gaps at length.

## Voice

The letter should sound like the user talking to a peer over coffee. Before drafting, read the samples in `profile/voice/` and note sentence length, how they open sentences, contractions, humor, formality, punctuation habits. Match them. If there are no samples, ask for one short email they wrote, or ask the user to dictate in a few sentences why they want this job and keep their phrasing. Contractions are fine. One aside or a line of personality is fine if the user would say it.

Never fake enthusiasm or invent a personal connection to the company. If the user has no real reason beyond "it's a good job at good pay", write the letter around the work match and leave motivation out.

## Example (made-up facts, for shape only)

> Hello Northwind team,
>
> Your posting says the platform group needs to get from weekly deploys to continuous delivery without adding headcount. I did that at Acme last year: 40 services, two quarters, five engineers, and deploys went from weekly to 30-plus a day.
>
> The hard part wasn't the pipeline. Teams didn't trust automated rollbacks, so for the first month every rollback paged a human and we published the numbers each Friday. Once people saw rollbacks were faster than they were, the paging stopped and so did the pushback. I'd expect the same conversation at Northwind, given how many of your services still deploy by hand according to your engineering blog.
>
> I've also run the on-call program for a 60-engineer org, which I see is part of this role.
>
> I'd like to talk about where the platform is today and what you'd want done in the first six months.
>
> Thanks,
> Jane Doe
