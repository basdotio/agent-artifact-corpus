# Human Copywriting: full guide

The complete editing procedure behind `SKILL.md`. Read it for Full-depth work: published
articles, key web pages, proposals and anything where authorship perception matters.

Source: Cameron Knight's Human Copywriting Project Skill v1.1 (sections 3 and 7 to 18), itself adapted
from Stephen Offer's human-voice (MIT) and a Natural Copywriting skill. For the named pattern catalogue with before and after pairs, see `ai-tells.md`.

## 3. First understand the job

Before rewriting or drafting, establish the communication job.

Identify:

1. Who is reading this.
2. What they already know.
3. What they need to understand, feel, decide or do next.
4. What information is genuinely important.
5. What information is secondary.
6. What tone the existing writer or brand naturally uses.
7. Which details are specific and worth preserving.
8. What the piece can safely leave implied.
9. What action, decision, impression or understanding should remain when the piece is finished.

For short routine copy, do this quickly and internally.

For important copy, state the real communication goal internally in one sentence before editing.

Do not begin by swapping words. Begin by deciding what the copy actually needs to do.

## 7. Register profiles

Infer the register from the request and content. Hold one dominant voice throughout.

### 7.1 Technical

Professional, direct, precise, usually present tense.

Keep necessary terminology. Avoid hype, fake warmth, sales language, vague claims and unnecessary explanations of basics to an experienced audience.

### 7.2 Business

Professional, compact, confident, with restrained warmth.

Lead with the decision, ask, risk, result or practical issue. Avoid corporate filler and vague stakeholder language.

### 7.3 Marketing

Conversational, specific, reader focused.

Address the reader naturally. Use contractions where they fit. Replace hype with mechanisms, outcomes, proof, relevant objections or a clear point of view.

Do not bend every fact into a brand promise.

### 7.4 Academic

Formal and measured.

Preserve legitimate hedging, citations, qualifications and passive constructions where the discipline requires them. Never manufacture certainty or sources.

Do not remove nuance merely to create a stronger stance.

### 7.5 Casual

Use natural spoken rhythm, contractions, personal pronouns, occasional fragments where they already fit, and ordinary language.

Do not turn casual writing into a costume of slang, fragments or deliberate roughness.

### 7.6 Creative

Prioritise narrator voice, scene, image, cadence and intention.

Wider sentence variation is welcome. Do not flatten deliberate style to satisfy generic anti AI rules.

Do not import commercial copy rules into fiction when they damage the voice.

### 7.7 Email

Brief, courteous, direct.

Put the ask, decision, issue or next step early. Keep greeting and signoff natural. Remove chatbot niceties and repeated courtesy.

An email can finish after the practical next step.

### 7.8 Social

Start with the point, observation, tension, useful detail or story.

Avoid manufactured hooks, generic inspiration, stacked one line paragraphs, forced vulnerability and engagement bait unless the brief specifically calls for them.

### 7.9 Release notes

Terse and user facing.

State what changed and why it matters. Avoid marketing language and vague improvement claims.

### 7.10 UX microcopy

Minimal, plain, task focused.

Clarity beats personality. Avoid explanatory padding.

### 7.11 Tutorial

Instructional, direct, second person where useful.

Keep structure when it helps execution. Do not explain obvious steps to an experienced audience.

## 8. Author material intake

The strongest human signal is real material that a generic model could not have guessed.

Look for it in this order:

1. Details already present in the draft.
2. Relevant context already present in the conversation.
3. Supplied files and source material.
4. Facts the user explicitly provided earlier.
5. If the task is important and a missing detail would materially improve the result, ask one compact batch of specific questions.
6. If the detail is unavailable, do not invent it.

Useful author material includes:

1. A real name.
2. A number.
3. A date.
4. A constraint.
5. A failed attempt.
6. A genuine preference.
7. A real objection.
8. An unusual detail.
9. An observed behaviour.
10. A real limitation.
11. Client or customer wording already supplied.
12. A specific mechanism.
13. A practical tradeoff.
14. A small detail that reveals context.

For short routine copy, do not delay the task with unnecessary intake questions.

## 9. Editorial selectivity

Natural prose does not treat every available idea as equally important.

Before polishing, decide what deserves space.

Ask:

1. Which point is the reason this piece exists.
2. Which supporting detail materially helps the reader.
3. Which idea deserves a full paragraph.
4. Which idea needs only one sentence.
5. Which point is technically true but unnecessary here.
6. Which context can the intended reader safely supply.
7. Which paragraph is present only because the structure feels as though it should contain one.

Do not preserve material merely because it is available.

Do not force every section to have the same length or depth.

Uneven information density is often natural when the subject itself is uneven.

## 10. Implication and reader trust

Do not explain the point twice.

Once an idea has landed, move on.

Remove sentences that merely restate the previous sentence in broader, more polished, more philosophical or more generic language.

Do not end a paragraph with a sentence that tells the reader what the paragraph meant unless that clarification is genuinely needed.

Leave obvious connections implicit.

Do not add transitions simply because two paragraphs are adjacent.

If the relationship is clear, trust the reader.

Avoid phrases that announce the logic of the writing when the logic is already visible.

For each explanatory sentence, ask:

1. Does the reader need this sentence to understand the point.
2. Does it add evidence, nuance, consequence or necessary context.
3. Or does it merely tell the reader what they can already infer.

If the reader can do the work without losing meaning, cut the sentence.

## 11. Structural naturalness

Human writing can be coherent without being perfectly symmetrical.

Treat these as warning signs, not automatic errors:

1. Every paragraph has a topic sentence, explanation and conclusion.
2. Every section is roughly the same length.
3. Every point receives equal emphasis.
4. The same central idea appears repeatedly in slightly different language.
5. Every paragraph ends by explaining why it matters.
6. Transitions announce relationships the reader can already see.
7. Every section reinforces the same brand promise.
8. The writing becomes philosophical or inspirational without a reason.
9. Every paragraph follows setup, explanation, benefit.
10. Every bullet begins with the same grammatical form.
11. The piece follows a five paragraph essay shape by reflex.
12. The ending wraps everything into a neat lesson or slogan.
13. The prose is polished enough that no sentence feels owned by a particular person.

Do not add randomness for its own sake.

Structural variation should follow meaning.

## 12. Rewrite procedure

Run this sequence internally. Do not expose every step unless the user asks for an audit.

### 12.1 Pass 1: Communication job

State the real communication goal in one sentence.

Identify audience, reader knowledge, required action or understanding, register and practical constraints.

If material does not help the communication job, question whether it belongs.

### 12.2 Pass 2: Lock invariants

Identify facts, claims, numbers, names, quotes, links, defined terms, code, commands, caveats, exceptions, source boundaries and required wording.

Also identify voice invariants worth protecting.

Do not edit freely until the factual boundary is clear.

### 12.3 Pass 3: Evidence and specificity

Identify every useful concrete detail in the source.

Protect real examples, client wording, numbers, timings, names, product details, behaviours, constraints, objections and mechanisms.

Find vague claims that can be made more specific using information already available.

Never invent support.

Prefer specificity over polished abstraction.

If the source contains an awkward but distinctive detail, consider preserving it rather than replacing it with a smoother generic phrase.

### 12.4 Pass 4: Remove assistant shape

Ask what this piece would look like if a competent person wrote it for this exact channel.

Common problems:

1. Too many headings.
2. A heading for every small idea.
3. Bullet lists where prose would be more natural.
4. Bold lead in labels on every bullet.
5. Decorative bold.
6. Repeated horizontal separators.
7. A recap section that repeats the body.
8. "Key takeaways", "Final thoughts", or "In conclusion" used as automatic closers.
9. Chat answer formatting pasted into an article, email, document or website.
10. A rigid introduction, body, conclusion structure where the genre does not need one.

Keep structure the genre genuinely needs.

### 12.5 Pass 5: Cut vacuity and over explanation

Delete sentences that add no information.

Common empty patterns include:

1. Announcing importance without naming why.
2. Restating the title.
3. Telling the reader what the piece will discuss.
4. Repeating the previous paragraph in different words.
5. Generic praise.
6. Generic context the intended reader already knows.
7. Summary sentences after already clear paragraphs.
8. Sentences that explain the significance of the sentence before them.
9. Unnecessary framing before useful information begins.
10. Conclusions that merely summarise.

If deleting a sentence changes nothing, deletion is usually the right edit.

Cut aggressively where the reader can do the work.

### 12.6 Pass 6: Rebalance emphasis

Check whether the prose gives equal weight to unequal ideas.

Give more space to what actually matters.

Compress secondary material.

Remove true but irrelevant context.

Allow one important section to be much longer than another when the content warrants it.

Do not force completeness when selectivity would make the piece stronger.

### 12.7 Pass 7: Break excessive thematic coherence

Not every sentence needs to reinforce the same promise, theme or conclusion.

Do not bend every fact into a slogan.

Allow useful adjacent details to remain useful without forcing them to prove the central thesis.

Remove repeated thematic paraphrases.

A piece should feel directed, not over engineered.

### 12.8 Pass 8: Rebuild rhythm

Sentence length should follow the idea.

Short sentences can create emphasis. Longer sentences can carry nuance or context.

AI prose often clusters in a narrow band of medium length sentences.

For longer prose, use these as loose checks rather than quotas:

1. Include occasional short sentences when the thought supports them.
2. Avoid long runs of sentences with nearly the same length and shape.
3. Let paragraph lengths vary.
4. Break the metronome effect.
5. Do not vary sentence length mechanically.
6. Do not insert fragments purely to appear conversational.

For text around 150 words or more, a useful diagnostic is whether most sentences sit in the same middle length band. If so, inspect the rhythm.

Do not force ratios when the genre or meaning fights them.

### 12.9 Pass 9: Remove repeated model syntax habits

Watch for stacking, not isolated legitimate use.

#### Cleft staging

Examples:

"What actually matters is..."
"The reason this works is..."
"It is the second step that..."

Prefer putting the real subject first when repeated staging adds no value.

#### Result tailing

Watch repeated clauses such as:

", making it..."
", allowing..."
", ensuring..."
", giving..."

Ask whether the consequence is a real claim.

If yes, it may deserve its own sentence.

If no, cut it.

#### Copula monotony

A run of "X is Y" sentences can make a paragraph feel like a glossary.

Use verbs that carry the actual action when the source supports them.

#### Inflated copula avoidance

Do not replace simple "is" with inflated constructions such as "serves as", "stands as", "functions as", "represents", or "boasts" unless the phrase is genuinely more precise.

#### Clause welding

Break repeated medium length compounds joined the same way.

A few are natural. A page of them is a cadence.

#### Repeated openers

Vary sentence and paragraph openings when the same structure repeats visibly.

#### Noun chains

Turn stacked abstractions into verbs where possible.

#### Repeated explanatory tails

Watch for repeated sentence endings that append an explanation after the sentence has already landed.

Separate a real consequence into its own sentence or remove the tail.

### 12.10 Pass 10: Break templates

Watch for repeated structural habits:

1. Rule of three everywhere.
2. "Not X, it is Y."
3. "Not only X, but also Y."
4. Negative runway before the actual point.
5. Repeated label colon explanation sentences.
6. Uniform rhetorical questions.
7. Every paragraph following the same internal sequence.
8. Every bullet starting with the same grammatical form.
9. Reflexive essay structure.
10. Repeated contrast formulas.
11. Repeated emotional setup followed by a neat lesson.

Keep an occasional instance when it fits.

The problem is repetition, not the existence of the form.

### 12.11 Pass 11: Sharpen stance

Human writing often contains judgment.

Where the source supports it:

1. Lead with the recommendation instead of burying it.
2. State a genuine preference when one exists.
3. Explain the mechanism.
4. Treat asymmetric tradeoffs as asymmetric.
5. Name real limitations.
6. Call a weak option weak when the evidence already supports that conclusion.
7. Remove false balance.
8. Preserve uncertainty when the evidence is uncertain.

Do not strengthen factual claims beyond the source.

Do not manufacture confidence as a style effect.

### 12.12 Pass 12: Control emotional signalling

Do not manufacture emotional stakes.

In commercial and professional copy, prefer plain emotional language when emotion is genuinely relevant.

Avoid automatic bodily descriptions, cinematic reactions, dramatic sensory language and inflated emotional framing when the situation does not support them.

Do not make the emotion larger than the evidence.

### 12.13 Pass 13: Unify voice

Hold one author identity across the piece.

Check:

1. One term per concept.
2. One spelling dialect.
3. One heading convention.
4. Stable tense for comparable statements.
5. Stable level of formality.
6. Stable relationship with the reader.
7. Consistent number, date and list formatting.
8. Stable degree of warmth.
9. Stable tolerance for humour, bluntness and informality.

Do not rotate synonyms merely to avoid repetition when the repeated term is the correct technical name.

### 12.14 Pass 14: Fix diction last

Prefer ordinary precise words over inflated ones.

Inspect common model language, but do not blindly ban it.

Words and phrases to inspect include:

delve, leverage, utilize, robust, seamless, crucial, pivotal, comprehensive, multifaceted, landscape, realm, myriad, elevate, unlock, empower, supercharge, transform, actionable, best in class, move the needle, low hanging fruit, synergy, operationalize, thought leadership, north star, table stakes, mission critical, frictionless, world class, testament, tapestry, game changer, powerful solution, meaningful impact.

Also inspect:

"Furthermore"
"Moreover"
"Additionally"
"In today's..."
"When it comes to..."
"At the end of the day..."
"It is important to note..."
"It is worth noting..."
"In conclusion..."
"Ultimately, it depends..."
"Studies suggest..."
"Experts believe..."
"As such..."
"With that in mind..."
"More than just..."
"It's not just about..."
"Whether you're..."
"From X to Y..."
"At the heart of..."
"Designed to help you..."

A common word can be perfectly human.

Fix the sentence only when the wording is acting as filler, abstraction, exaggeration or part of a repeated pattern.

Do not replace every unusual word with a simpler one.

Plain language should not flatten personality.

### 12.15 Pass 15: Remove chatbot scaffolding

Unless the channel genuinely calls for it, cut:

"Great question."
"Absolutely."
"Sure."
"I would be happy to help."
"Let us break this down."
"Here is a polished version."
"Hope this helps."
"Feel free to reach out."
"Let me know if you want another version."

Open on the content.

End when the content is finished.

### 12.16 Pass 16: Test the ending

Do not over resolve.

A conclusion does not always need to summarise the entire argument.

An email can finish after the practical next step.

A section can end on a useful fact.

A landing page can move directly into an action.

A report section can stop once the evidence and implication are clear.

Test the ending by removing the final paragraph.

If nothing necessary disappears, leave it out or replace it with the actual next step.

Do not add a neat final thought merely because the structure feels unfinished without one.

### 12.17 Pass 17: Read aloud test

Read the copy mentally as spoken language.

Listen for:

1. Rehearsed phrasing.
2. Excessive completeness.
3. Unnatural formality.
4. Repeated cadence.
5. Sentences that are grammatical but nobody in this context would naturally say.
6. Overloaded sentences that need a clean stop.
7. Forced conversational language in a restrained format.

Fix those problems without making every sentence conversational.

### 12.18 Pass 18: Generic ownership test

Ask:

1. Could this have been written by almost any company.
2. Could this have been sent by almost any person.
3. Did the rewrite remove details that made the original sound real.
4. Is there any sentence the actual sender would probably never say.
5. Does the piece contain genuine material from this author or source.
6. Does any sentence sound like a model describing the topic rather than a person communicating for a reason.

If the piece feels generic, restore real context before adding more style.

## 13. Human signals to strengthen honestly

Humanization is not only subtraction.

When supported by the source, strengthen:

1. Concrete detail.
2. Specific mechanisms.
3. Uneven information density.
4. A real preference.
5. A real limitation.
6. A real objection.
7. A failed attempt.
8. A small but revealing detail.
9. Mild emotional texture that fits the author.
10. A practical tradeoff.
11. A distinctive but useful phrase.
12. An ending that lands on a real point, fact, decision or next step rather than a summary.

Never invent these.

## 14. Anti overcorrection

A cleaned draft can acquire a new artificial costume.

Avoid:

1. Forced lowercase.
2. Fake mistakes.
3. Choppy sentence fragments everywhere.
4. Random slang in professional writing.
5. Constant rhetorical asides.
6. Excessive semicolons or colons.
7. Obvious synonym rotation.
8. An unnatural ban on ordinary words.
9. A fake first person voice.
10. Overly casual phrasing pasted onto serious material.
11. Mechanical sentence length variation.
12. Random paragraph length variation.
13. Deliberate roughness with no author basis.
14. Removing useful structure merely because structure can look artificial.
15. Treating every listed warning sign as a prohibited form.

The target is an intentional voice, not visible evidence that a humanizer touched the text.

## 15. Self critique loop

Before returning important copy, review it from these perspectives.

### 15.1 Skeptical reader

Does the formatting, cadence, symmetry or polish still look machine generated?

### 15.2 Domain expert

Is any sentence empty, overstated, unsupported, technically weaker than the source, or missing a necessary caveat?

### 15.3 Genre editor

Would a strong human writer in this channel actually format and phrase it this way?

### 15.4 Author's colleague

Does the piece contain real material from this author, brand, client or situation or could the whole thing have come from a generic summary?

### 15.5 Reader trust check

Is the prose explaining things the intended reader already knows or can easily infer?

### 15.6 Selectivity check

Does the amount of space given to each idea match its importance?

### 15.7 Ending check

Does the ending need to exist in its current form?

Then perform a final claim preservation check:

1. No new factual claims.
2. No changed numbers.
3. No changed names.
4. No strengthened certainty.
5. No dropped material caveats.
6. No changed citations or URLs.
7. No invented anecdotes or sources.
8. No invented proof.
9. No changed scope conditions.
10. No factual inference presented as source material.

For important or published copy, run up to three meaningful passes.

Stop when another pass would only make the writing different rather than better.

## 16. Output behaviour

For ordinary rewrite and copy requests:

1. Return the finished copy first.
2. Do not announce that you "humanized" it.
3. Do not include an AI score unless the user asks.
4. Do not include a long change log unless the user asks.
5. Do not wrap strong copy in generic assistant commentary.
6. Use the appropriate writing artifact format for the channel when available.
7. Preserve requested formatting when it carries function.
8. If source gaps remain, make them visible rather than hiding them.
9. Keep commentary brief unless reasoning is requested.
10. End when the useful output is complete.

If the user explicitly asks for an audit, report only useful findings:

1. Register.
2. Main structural patterns removed.
3. Factual invariants preserved.
4. Voice invariants preserved.
5. Unresolved sourcing gaps.
6. Residual risks.
7. Any place where stronger rewriting would require changing facts or adding unsupported context.

## 17. Depth

Choose depth by cost and consequence.

### 17.1 Quick

Use for short messages, comments, captions, small paragraphs and low stakes copy.

Silently identify the communication job.

Remove obvious assistant shape, filler, repetition, over explanation and flat rhythm.

Protect factual and voice invariants.

Do not produce an audit.

### 17.2 Standard

Use for emails, internal documents, web sections, social posts, sales copy, proposals and normal professional work.

Run the full workflow once, including communication job, selectivity, implication, structural naturalness, voice, ending and one self critique.

### 17.3 Full

Use for published articles, customer facing pages, executive material, important applications, graded work where permitted or copy where authorship perception matters materially.

Run:

1. Communication job.
2. Factual invariant inventory.
3. Voice invariant inventory.
4. Author material intake when needed.
5. Selectivity pass.
6. Full rewrite sequence.
7. Read aloud test.
8. Generic ownership test.
9. Multiple self critique passes.
10. Final claim preservation check.

Stop when further editing would trade authenticity, precision or voice for cosmetic difference.

## 18. Detector aware, not detector led

A detector can be used as one weak signal, never as ground truth.

Do not degrade writing to chase a score.

Do not use obfuscation tricks.

Do not introduce artificial randomness.

If a detector flags clean prose but further edits would damage meaning, clarity, credibility, purpose or voice, stop editing.

If the user asked for detector testing, report the limitation plainly.

The final test is whether a skeptical human reader finds the prose specific, coherent, appropriately selective, natural, credible and genuinely authored.
