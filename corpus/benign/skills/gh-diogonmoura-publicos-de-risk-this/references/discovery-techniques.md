# Discovery Techniques

Discovery answers "is this worth building?". Delivery answers "is this built well?".
They run **in parallel and continuously** — never as phases.

The point of every technique below is the same: buy evidence at the lowest possible
cost, before the expensive commitment.

## Framing — what problem, for whom

- **Opportunity assessment.** Four questions: what business objective is this for,
  how will we know we succeeded (the measure), what problem does it solve for the
  customer, and which customer specifically.
- **Customer letter.** Write the end state in the customer's own voice — a letter
  they would write once it works. If you cannot write the letter, you do not have
  the outcome.
- **Startup canvas.** For genuinely new bets, not for features.

## Planning

- **Story maps.** Lay the work out along the user's journey rather than as a flat list.
- **Reference customers.** A small set of real, named customers who commit to using
  the thing. Better than a survey of everyone.

## Ideation — where ideas actually come from

- Customer interviews (continuous, not a project)
- Concierge test: do the job manually yourself before automating it
- Hack days
- The support queue
- The data

Note the source: **engineers exposed to the problem** are the best source of
innovation the company has. Engineers handed a solution are not.

## Prototyping

Four kinds:
- **Feasibility** prototype — engineering answers "can we?"
- **User** prototype — a simulation, for usability
- **Live-data** prototype — real data, real traffic, to measure value
- **Hybrid** — combinations of the above

Properties that make a prototype a prototype: **low cost, fast cycle, and disposable
by definition.** A prototype you are reluctant to throw away is a product you built
by accident.

## Testing

| Testing for | How |
|---|---|
| Usability | A handful of users. Five is usually enough to find the serious problems. |
| Value (qualitative) | Prototype in front of real users; watch what they do, not what they say |
| Value (quantitative) | A/B test, fake door |
| Feasibility | Engineering, with time to actually look |
| Business viability | The internal stakeholders — legal, finance, sales, marketing, security |

## Cheapest-next-move heuristic

When a gate is blocked, do not ask "how do we prove this?" — ask **"what is the
cheapest thing that would change my mind?"** Rank by cost:

1. Look at data you already have
2. Ask five customers
3. Fake door
4. Concierge (do it by hand)
5. Live-data prototype
6. A/B test on a real surface

Take the cheapest one that could actually falsify the belief. A test that cannot
come back negative is not a test.
