# Teams, Roles and Culture

## The product team

Product manager, designer, engineers. Properties that make it a product team rather
than a group of people:

- **Its own mission** — a problem space, not a queue
- **Durable** — it persists; it is not assembled per project
- **Autonomous** — it can decide how to solve the problem
- **Accountable for an outcome**, not for a list

Remove any one of the four and the rest stop working. Autonomy without accountability
is chaos; accountability without autonomy is blame.

## The roles

**Product manager** — answers for **value** and **business viability**. Must be among
the best-informed people in the company on customers, data, the business and the
market. Explicitly *not* a project manager and *not* a requirements author. The test:
if the PM's contribution is scheduling and writing tickets, the role is vacant.

**Product designer** — answers for the **experience**. Involved from the start. A
designer brought in to make the finished thing pretty is a decorator, not a designer.

**Engineers** — answer for **feasibility**, and are the best source of innovation the
company has — *on the condition that they are exposed to the problem, not just the
solution.* This condition is the whole point and is the thing most often missing.

**Leadership** — the main job of the product leader and the technology leader is to
**develop people**, not to decide for them. Without this, autonomy becomes chaos and
the decisions climb back up the hierarchy.

## Strong team vs. weak team

The most-quoted passage of the book. The contrast, distilled:

| Strong team | Weak team |
|---|---|
| Gets problems | Gets features |
| Talks to customers directly, weekly | Hears about customers second-hand |
| Discovers before committing | Commits, then discovers it was wrong |
| Owns an outcome | Owns a delivery date |
| Engineers see the problem | Engineers see the spec |
| Disagrees with stakeholders, with evidence | Takes orders and resents them |
| Kills its own ideas when the data says so | Ships everything it starts |

## Why innovation is lost — diagnostic checklist

- Risk aversion (the cost of a failed experiment is treated as the cost of a failed product)
- Stakeholders dictating solutions rather than stating problems
- No direct access to customers
- Teams organised by **feature or component** rather than by **problem**

## Why velocity is lost — diagnostic checklist

- Technical debt
- Dependencies between teams
- Decisions that always climb the hierarchy

That third one has a specific diagnostic: count the decisions per week that go up a
level. Then ask, for each, whether it went up for lack of **context** or lack of
**competence**. Almost always it is context — and context is a leadership failure,
not a team failure.

## What a strong culture combines

Urgency, experimentation, and learning — with **tolerance for error in discovery**
and **intolerance for error in delivery**. Confusing the two is fatal in both
directions: a team punished for a failed experiment stops experimenting; a team
forgiven for a broken deploy stops being careful.
