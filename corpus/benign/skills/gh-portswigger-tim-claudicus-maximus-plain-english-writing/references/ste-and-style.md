# Wording guidance (paraphrased, with reasons)

This is a working summary in our own words. The STE part was checked against ASD-STE100 Issue 9 (2025-01-15). It is not the specification. It does not include the approved-word dictionary. If the user needs certified STE (for example for aerospace or defence documentation), point them to the official specification and dictionary.

Each rule has a reason, marked *Why*. Use the reason to decide whether the rule applies. If the reason does not apply to the text in front of you, the rule is a guideline you can set aside. Some reasons come from the specification. Others are our own explanation.

## From ASD-STE100, Issue 9 (rule numbers for reference)

Words (section 1)
- 1.1–1.4: Use approved words, in the approved part of speech, with the approved meaning, in the approved form. *Why:* one word with one meaning leaves the reader nothing to guess. Many readers are not native English speakers, and they cannot rely on context to pick the right meaning.
- 1.5–1.6: Words that are not in the dictionary are allowed only as technical nouns or technical verbs in the approved categories. *Why:* a reader needs the exact name of a part or an action. Everything else can be said in ordinary words.
- 1.7, 1.13: Do not use a technical noun as a verb, or a technical verb as a noun. *Why:* a word that changes its part of speech changes its meaning, and readers and translators can miss the switch.
- 1.9–1.11: Choose short, easy technical nouns. Do not use slang, jargon or regional words. Use one term for one item. *Why:* slang and regional words are not known to every reader. Two names for one item make the reader think there are two items.
- 1.14: Use American spelling unless told otherwise. *Why:* one spelling means one form to learn. This skill follows the project's spelling instead.

Multi-word nouns (section 2)
- 2.1: No more than 3 words in a noun group. *Why:* in a long stack ("user session token validation error") the reader cannot tell which word describes which.
- 2.2: If a technical noun has more than 3 words, write it in full and make the relations clear. *Why:* short forms and stacks hide the relations between the words.

Verbs (section 3)
- 3.2: Use only the simple past, simple present, future, and a small set of forms (infinitive, imperative, past participle as an adjective). *Why:* fewer verb forms mean fewer meanings to work out, and simple tenses translate well.
- 3.3: Use the past participle as an adjective ("the closed valve"). *Why:* it is a short, clear way to describe a state.
- 3.4: Do not build complex verb constructions with auxiliary verbs. *Why:* strings such as "would have been being checked" are hard to parse and hard to translate.
- 3.5: Use the *-ing* form only as a technical noun, or as a modifier in one. *Why:* an *-ing* word can be a verb, an adjective or a noun. That causes ambiguity and long, tangled sentences.
- 3.6: Use the active voice. In descriptive writing, the passive is allowed only if the agent is unknown. *Why:* the active voice says who or what does the action. The passive can hide the actor, and the reader may not know who has to act.
- 3.7: Use a verb to describe an action, not a noun or another word. *Why:* "test the pump" is shorter and clearer than "carry out a test of the pump".

Sentences (section 4)
- 4.1: Short, clear sentences. *Why:* a short sentence holds one idea, so the reader can keep it in mind while reading.
- 4.2: Do not omit words or use contractions to make sentences shorter. *Why:* a short sentence with missing words is not easier to read. The reader has to guess the gap. Contractions are also harder for non-native readers and for translation.
- 4.3: Use a vertical list for complex text. *Why:* a list shows the structure that a long sentence hides.
- 4.4: Use connecting words to join related sentences. *Why:* words such as "because" and "then" show how two ideas relate.
- 4.5: Use an article (*the*, *a*, *an*) or a demonstrative (*this*, *these*) when applicable. *Why:* a missing article can change the meaning. "Remove the bolt and stop" is not the same as "Remove the bolt and the stop".

Procedures (section 5)
- 5.1: Maximum 20 words per sentence. *Why:* the reader is doing a task and glancing at the text. Short steps are quick to read and to remember.
- 5.2: One instruction per sentence, unless two actions happen at the same time. *Why:* the reader can do one thing, check it, and come back to the next. A step with two actions is easy to half-complete.
- 5.3: Imperative form. *Why:* a command is the shortest, most direct way to tell the reader what to do.
- 5.4: If the reader must know a condition first, start with it. *Why:* the reader may start the action before they read the condition.
- 5.5: Notes give information, not instructions. *Why:* a reader can skip a note as optional. An instruction hidden in a note may be missed, so put it in a step.

Descriptions (section 6)
- 6.1–6.2: Give information gradually. Use key words and phrases to give a logical structure. *Why:* too much at once overloads the reader. Signal words let the reader follow the order of ideas.
- 6.3: Maximum 25 words per sentence. *Why:* descriptions can hold more than steps, but long sentences still slow the reader down.
- 6.4–6.6: Paragraphs show related information, have one topic, and have no more than 6 sentences. *Why:* a paragraph is a chunk. A single topic lets the reader find and re-find information.

Safety instructions (section 7)
- 7.1: Use a level word such as WARNING or CAUTION. *Why:* the reader sees how serious the risk is before they read on.
- 7.2: Start with a clear command or condition. *Why:* the reader who reads only the first words still knows what to do or avoid.
- 7.3: Say what the risk or result is. *Why:* a reason makes the reader more likely to follow the instruction.

Punctuation (section 8)
- 8.1: Standard punctuation, but no semicolons. *Why:* a semicolon packs two ideas into one sentence and its use is not clear to every reader. Two sentences are clearer.
- 8.2: Hyphens connect words that are directly related. *Why:* a hyphen shows which words belong together ("high-pressure pump").

Practices (section 9)
- 9.1: If a word-for-word swap does not work, rewrite the sentence. *Why:* swapping words in a bad sentence gives a bad sentence in simpler words.
- 9.3: Do not make phrasal verbs from two words. *Why:* the meaning of "take off" or "set up" changes with the particle, and a reader cannot work it out from the parts.
- 9.4: Keep a consistent style. *Why:* a consistent text is predictable, and predictable text is easier to read.
- General recommendations:
  - Use "that" after verbs such as "make sure". *Why:* it marks where the main clause ends and the next begins. Many other languages need the equivalent word, so it also helps translation.
  - Use pronouns with care. *Why:* the reader must be able to tell what "it" or "they" refers to.
  - Avoid "this" alone before a verb. Name the thing. *Why:* "This causes a fault" makes the reader search for what "this" is.
  - Watch for false friends. *Why:* some English words look like words in other languages but mean something different.
  - No Latin abbreviations (*e.g.*, *i.e.*, *etc.*). *Why:* readers who do not know them are confused. English words are clear to everyone.
  - Use inclusive language and take care with possessive forms. *Why:* the text should not exclude readers, and possessives can hide who owns what.

STE modal verbs: the dictionary maps *may* to *can*, and *shall* and *should* to *must*. So strict STE has no lowercase "should". *Why:* "may" can mean permission or possibility, and "should" leaves it unclear how strong the requirement is.

RFC 2119 (with RFC 8174) is a separate convention. It defines MUST, MUST NOT, REQUIRED, SHALL, SHALL NOT, SHOULD, SHOULD NOT, RECOMMENDED, MAY and OPTIONAL as requirement terms, but only when written in capitals. It is allowed in this skill for specifications and similar requirement text. Keep the capitals, and do not use the words as casual advice. *Why:* the standard gives each word one exact meaning, which removes the ambiguity that STE avoids by banning the words. The capitals show that the word is the defined term.

## Vocabulary habits (general, not the STE dictionary)

*Why:* short, everyday words are understood by more readers, including non-native speakers and people in a hurry. A long word rarely adds meaning.

| Avoid | Use |
|---|---|
| utilise, employ, leverage | use |
| commence, initiate | start, begin |
| terminate | end, stop |
| ascertain | find out, check |
| in order to | to |
| prior to | before |
| subsequent to | after |
| in the event that | if |
| a number of | some, several (or the number) |
| make a decision | decide |
| perform an installation | install |
| is able to | can |

## From Strunk, *The Elements of Style* (paraphrased)

- Make each paragraph the unit of a single topic. Begin with the topic sentence, end with a sentence that closes it. *Why:* the reader knows what the paragraph is about from its first sentence, and can skip it if it is not relevant.
- Use the active voice. *Why:* it is direct and shows who acts.
- Put statements in positive form. *Why:* "He forgot" is firmer than "He did not remember". A positive statement tells the reader what is, not only what is not.
- Use definite, specific, concrete language. *Why:* the reader can picture it or act on it. Vague words ("some problems", "in some cases") give the reader nothing to use.
- Omit needless words. *Why:* every word costs the reader some attention. A word that adds nothing takes it for no return.
- Avoid a series of loose sentences. Vary sentence form when the rhythm turns monotonous, but do not decorate. *Why:* a long run of the same pattern tires the reader. The variety is for the reader's benefit, not for style.
- Express co-ordinate ideas in similar form. *Why:* matching form shows the reader that the ideas are equal and lets them see the pattern.
- Keep related words together. *Why:* the reader links words by closeness. A modifier far from its word is easy to misread.
- In summaries, keep to one tense. *Why:* a switch of tense suggests a change in time where there is none.
- Place the emphatic words of a sentence at the end. *Why:* the end of a sentence is the part that the reader remembers most.
- Common traps: "the fact that", "the reason is because", "different than", "however" at the start of a sentence. *Why:* the first is wordy. The second says "because" twice. The third and fourth are often used loosely and make the sentence heavier than it needs to be.

## From gov.uk content guidance (paraphrased)

- Write for the user's task, not the organisation's structure. *Why:* readers come with a job to do. They do not know, or care, how the organisation is arranged.
- Use words the reader uses. Avoid jargon, buzzwords and formal language ("we will", not "the department shall"). *Why:* readers search for and recognise their own words. Formal language sounds distant and makes them work harder.
- Keep sentences under 25 words. Aim for 5 to 20 in key text. *Why:* long sentences are harder to follow, especially for readers with a low reading level or a learning difficulty.
- Tell people what to do. Use "you", not "the applicant". *Why:* speaking to the reader directly makes it clear who has to act.
- Put the main point in the title and first paragraph. *Why:* many readers stop early. They must get the answer before they do.
- Use headings and lists to help scanning, but keep them short and descriptive. *Why:* people scan for the part they need. A heading works only if it says what is under it.
- Write dates as "1 March 2026". Use "to" in ranges. *Why:* "1/3/26" means different dates in different countries. A dash in a range can be read aloud wrongly by a screen reader.
- Spell out abbreviations on first use, unless they are widely known. *Why:* the reader may not know them, and the abbreviation may mean something else to them.
- Avoid these words:
  - "click here". *Why:* the link text tells the reader nothing when it is read out of context, for example in a list of links from a screen reader. It also assumes a mouse.
  - "please". *Why:* it adds a word with no meaning, and in a step it can make a required action sound optional.
  - "very". *Why:* it adds emphasis but no information, and it weakens the word next to it. Use a stronger word or a fact.
  - "easy", "simple". *Why:* they are opinions. What is easy for you may be hard for the reader, and they may feel that they have failed. Say what to do and let the reader judge.
- Test with real readers when it matters. *Why:* the writer knows too much to see where a reader will stumble.
