## Value and customer

Start with the job, not the product:

- What progress is the customer trying to make?
- What is currently expensive, slow, risky, embarrassing, uncertain, or inconvenient?
- What alternative is used now, including doing nothing?
- Who uses, chooses, influences, pays, and bears risk? They may be different people.
- Is the purchase frequent, urgent, high-value, identity-driven, or regulated?

Value created is not automatically value captured. A business captures value only when it can charge, collect, retain enough margin, and prevent easy replacement.

## Unit economics

Use the smallest repeatable unit: one order, customer, ride, store, seat, loan, room-night, or creator.

Core calculations:

- `revenue = price × paid volume`
- `gross profit = revenue - direct cost of goods/services`
- `contribution = revenue - variable costs attributable to the unit`
- `contribution margin = contribution / revenue`
- `break-even volume = fixed costs / contribution per unit`
- `take rate = platform revenue / transaction value`
- `approx. LTV = contribution per period × expected retained periods`
- `CAC payback periods = acquisition cost / contribution per period`

State what is excluded. LTV is fragile when retention, discounting, support cost, or repeat behavior is uncertain.

Ask whether growth improves or worsens the unit. Negative contribution cannot be repaired by volume unless price, cost, mix, or behavior changes.

## Cash and working capital

Profit answers whether value exceeded cost under accounting rules. Cash answers whether obligations can be paid now.

Map:

- when customers pay;
- when suppliers and workers are paid;
- how long inventory sits;
- how long receivables remain unpaid;
- refund, warranty, reward, and tax liabilities;
- debt maturity and withdrawal rights.

Useful measures:

- `cash conversion cycle = inventory days + receivable days - payable days`
- `inventory turns = annual cost of goods / average inventory`
- `free cash flow ≈ operating cash flow - capital expenditure`

Negative working capital can be an advantage when customers pay before suppliers, but it becomes dangerous if growth slows, refunds rise, or supplier terms tighten.

Never call customer deposits, gift-card balances, insurance premiums, escrow funds, or merchant settlements “free money.” Identify the obligation and legal restrictions.

## Pricing

Separate:

- cost-plus pricing;
- competitor-referenced pricing;
- value-based pricing;
- segmented/versioned pricing;
- subscription and usage pricing;
- auction or dynamic pricing;
- bundling and unbundling;
- penetration, skimming, and loss-leader pricing.

Test willingness to pay, not merely stated interest. A discount can increase conversion while destroying contribution margin or teaching customers to wait.

Show price architecture as a story:

`entry offer -> core profit item -> repeat purchase -> premium upgrade -> retention benefit`

## Growth and compounding

Name the engine:

- paid acquisition;
- salesforce;
- organic search/content;
- referral and virality;
- distribution partners;
- retail density;
- marketplace network effect;
- franchising/licensing;
- product-led growth;
- acquisition and consolidation;
- reinvestment of retained earnings.

Distinguish:

- **linear growth**: more input produces proportionally more output;
- **economies of scale**: average cost falls with volume;
- **learning curve**: cost or quality improves through accumulated experience;
- **network effect**: value to a user rises with other participants;
- **compounding**: retained gains expand the base producing later gains;
- **virality**: existing users directly cause new-user acquisition.

Use a flywheel only when each step causally strengthens the next. Write the evidence for every arrow.

## Moats and leverage

Possible advantages:

- lower structural cost;
- brand and trust;
- proprietary distribution;
- network effect;
- switching cost and workflow integration;
- scale economies;
- unique data or learning system;
- regulated license or scarce location;
- intellectual property;
- supply access;
- community and identity;
- process quality and execution speed.

A moat protects value capture; it does not guarantee customer value or ethical conduct.

Leverage amplifies output per unit of founder time:

- capital buys assets and inventory;
- labor systems coordinate people;
- software replicates decisions;
- media replicates messages;
- brand and trust reduce persuasion cost;
- partners supply distribution or capacity.

Always show the compensation and downside attached to leverage.

## Incentives and game theory

Ask:

- What behavior is each party rewarded for?
- Which metric can be gamed?
- Who has information the other side lacks?
- Can the agent profit while the principal loses?
- Does insurance or a guarantee create moral hazard?
- Does one party hold the other hostage after investment?
- What happens after competitors copy the move?
- What happens when all participants pursue the same strategy?

Useful concepts:

- principal-agent problem;
- adverse selection;
- moral hazard;
- tragedy of the commons;
- prisoner's dilemma;
- hold-up problem;
- winner's curse;
- signaling and screening;
- switching cost;
- regulatory capture.

Explain concepts through a transaction, not as vocabulary lessons.

## Risk and decision quality

Separate risk from uncertainty. Risk can be roughly modeled; uncertainty may lack stable probabilities.

Before recommending a model, test:

- base rate and survivorship bias;
- maximum plausible loss;
- time to cash recovery;
- customer and supplier concentration;
- single-platform or single-policy dependence;
- key-person dependence;
- operating leverage and fixed commitments;
- liquidity under a 30% demand fall;
- fraud, quality, cyber, legal, and reputation exposure;
- reversibility and option value.

Use three scenarios: downside, base, upside. Do not use one-point forecasts as certainty.

Decision tools:

- small reversible experiment before irreversible commitment;
- pre-mortem: assume failure and list causes;
- kill criteria: define when to stop before investing;
- margin of safety: require room for error;
- sensitivity analysis: change the most uncertain variable;
- opportunity cost: compare with the best realistic alternative;
- sunk-cost rule: past unrecoverable spend does not justify future spend;
- second-order effects: ask what happens after the immediate result.

## Short-video explanation templates

### Who really earns?

> The visible seller earns `[margin]`, but the durable profit pool belongs to `[control point]` because it owns `[distribution/trust/data/standard/capital]`.

### Revenue is not cash

> Orders doubled, profit looked positive, but customer cash arrived after supplier and payroll deadlines. The business did not die from lack of sales; it died from timing.

### Growth trap

> Every new customer added revenue and a larger loss. The problem was not insufficient scale; the repeatable unit was broken.

### Float

> The company received money today and paid obligations later. The interval had value, but the money remained attached to a promise.

### Network effect

> One user made the product useful; the next user made it more useful to everyone. The same network also made a failure systemic.

### Compounding

> The first gain was small. What mattered was that it was retained, improved, and returned to a larger base instead of being consumed.
