The estimates come from automated transcription and half-second visual-difference sampling. Use them as production baselines, not exact prescriptions.

Shared format:

- Begin with a black screen and the series phrase “today's life scenario is...” followed by a provocative identity.
- Narrate almost entirely in second person and present tense.
- Use concrete sensory objects and times instead of abstract summaries.
- Place large white Chinese subtitles with black outline at the bottom.
- Place a small fictional-content/value disclaimer at the top.
- Use mostly static AI images with slow movement, cross-dissolves, and a new composition about every seven seconds.
- Let one recurring object return at the collapse: notes, a lamp, a cigarette, a gift box, a game inventory, fingerprints, or a door knock.
- End with an aphorism or blessing that viewers can repeat or forward.

## Video 1: performative effort

### Story engine

The protagonist carefully performs the appearance of studying: arriving early but sleeping until classmates arrive, copying colorful notes without thinking, studying where teachers can see, refusing to turn off classroom lights during a film, posting late-night desk photos, and judging classmates who relax. The high-stakes exam exposes that the visible signals did not produce ability.

### Human drives

- status through moral superiority;
- approval from teachers and peers;
- self-deception through visible effort;
- resentment after reality rejects the performance.

### Retention devices

- Each new scene is a recognizable “fake diligence” behavior.
- The viewer anticipates exposure and mentally compares the character with people they know.
- Specific props make the satire shareable: five-color highlighters, vocabulary book during PE, desk lamp, coffee, social post, and “do not turn off the light.”
- The exam acts as an objective market test that ignores signaling.

### Commercial adaptation

Use for:

- fake busyness versus customer value;
- beautiful business plans without sales;
- vanity metrics versus retained customers;
- office presenteeism versus measurable output;
- content creators buying equipment instead of publishing;
- “entrepreneur identity” consumption versus unit economics.

Core commercial lesson: markets may notice signals temporarily, but repeat purchases, cash collection, and outcomes eventually test real value.

### Caution

Do not turn the story into contempt for struggling people. Diagnose the incentive and avoidance behavior, then give a small corrective action.

## Video 2: relationship standards shaped by short video

### Story engine

A stable but ordinary relationship is reframed as inadequate after the protagonist repeatedly watches staged relationship tests. Algorithmic recommendations amplify grievance. She copies the tests into real life, demands gifts and ritual proof, and loses a caring partner. A later reveal shows that the influencer's scenes and transfers were staged.

### Human drives

- romantic validation and comparison;
- fear of being insufficiently loved;
- aspiration toward a polished life;
- anger generated by simple slogans;
- algorithmic reinforcement.

### Retention devices

- Warm ordinary details establish what is at risk: waiting at the subway, remembering an allergy, sharing inexpensive food, repairing a lamp.
- Viral slogans repeat and acquire a more destructive meaning each time.
- Escalation moves from food to gifts, debt, conflict rules, and separation.
- The staged-content reveal creates a second villain: the attention business profiting from dissatisfaction.

### Commercial adaptation

Use for:

- beauty or luxury marketing that monetizes comparison;
- relationship creators selling courses through anxiety;
- algorithms turning mild dissatisfaction into demand;
- status consumption financed by credit;
- products that manufacture a problem before selling the cure.

Map both flows: viewer attention to platform/creator and money from anxious consumer to product/course/credit provider.

### Caution

Avoid gender war. Make both people credible, distinguish healthy standards from manufactured tests, and explain the business incentive instead of blaming one gender.

## Video 3: humiliation, wealth, and brotherhood

### Story engine

A poor nineteen-year-old is humiliated at a village banquet, leaves with almost nothing, spends nine hard years building a company, buys a Maybach, returns home, ignores flattering relatives, and offers a real opportunity to the childhood friend who once helped him buy a train ticket.

### Human drives

- status restoration and revenge fantasy;
- visible proof of success;
- loyalty and repayment;
- masculine brotherhood;
- hope of escaping a low-status origin.

### Retention devices

- Opens with humiliation and quickly promises a dramatic identity reversal.
- Luxury details provide sensory reward: showroom, receipt, closing door, engine, village arrival.
- The emotional climax is not the car but recognizing the loyal friend.
- The ending directly blesses “brothers” watching, giving viewers a reason to forward it to a friend.

### Commercial adaptation

Use the status-reversal frame only when the skipped middle is filled with an actual business mechanism:

- how the first customer was won;
- how an offer changed;
- how gross margin and cash collection improved;
- which distribution channel scaled;
- when hiring became rational;
- which risk nearly broke the company.

Make the final symbol a consequence of cash-generating capability, not the lesson itself. A stronger ending is helping an early ally enter the system with clear responsibilities and incentives.

### Caution

The source skips nine years of business causality and risks equating dignity with a luxury car. Do not reproduce that omission.

## Video 4: game addiction and real-world bankruptcy

### Story engine

A student downloads a game out of boredom. An early rare reward creates intense excitement. Mastery, loot, rankings, game-specific language, and intermittent rewards gradually replace study, friends, hygiene, sleep, and a relationship. He wins a valuable game round at the exact moment his partner ends the relationship, turning virtual success into real-world loss.

### Human drives

- intermittent reward and near-miss excitement;
- competence and mastery;
- insider identity and player belonging;
- scarcity and collection;
- sunk cost and loss chasing.

### Retention devices

- Uses accurate player vocabulary and recognizable items as membership signals.
- Shows the first jackpot in sensory detail before showing addiction.
- Creates two parallel scoreboards: virtual inventory rising, real life deteriorating.
- The anniversary deadline collides with a prolonged match.
- The line “rich in the game, bankrupt in reality” compresses the entire story.

### Commercial adaptation

Use for:

- attention and game economies;
- blind-box, lottery-like, live-stream gifting, shopping, or trading loops;
- platforms that convert uncertain rewards into session length and purchases;
- content creators confusing followers with income;
- entrepreneurs chasing dashboard metrics while customers or family leave.

Show the commercial loop:

`uncertain reward -> longer session -> more data/social pressure -> higher conversion -> platform revenue -> user opportunity cost`

### Caution

Do not treat serious addiction as solved by closing the computer once. Include friction, replacement routines, accountability, limits, and professional support when appropriate.

## Video 5: exam-theft thriller

### Story engine

A repeat candidate becomes convinced that effort is insufficient and seeks an illegal shortcut. The story follows surveillance, preparation, execution, score control, an unexpected replacement exam, fingerprint anxiety, and a quiet police visit. The plan fails because the protagonist cannot control the full system.

### Human drives

- desperation for escape;
- “smart shortcut” fantasy;
- control through detailed planning;
- fear of detection;
- justice and inevitable consequences.

### Retention devices

- Procedural specificity makes the plan feel real.
- Clocks, blind spots, physical tools, weather, fingerprints, and quiet knocks form a thriller grammar.
- The protagonist apparently succeeds several times, extending uncertainty.
- The exam replacement is a strong system-level reversal.
- Consequences arrive calmly rather than through a loud chase, which increases dread.

### Commercial adaptation

Transform the operational crime details into a legal commercial audit or fraud-autopsy story:

- fake orders used to attract investment;
- revenue recognized before cash arrives;
- inventory hidden before due diligence;
- stolen customer lists that fail because relationships do not transfer;
- platform arbitrage that disappears after a policy change;
- a counterfeit supply chain exposed by one traceable inconsistency.

Focus on red flags, detection, victims, and consequences. Do not provide executable instructions for theft, fraud, evasion, or evidence destruction.

## Cross-sample lessons

### What repeatedly works

- Provocative identity is understood immediately.
- Second-person narration turns explanation into simulation.
- The protagonist's first choice is understandable, even when later choices are harmful.
- One psychological reward starts the loop: approval, validation, revenge, rare loot, or control.
- Costs escalate across multiple life domains, not money alone.
- An early object or phrase returns with reversed meaning.
- Apparent success and real failure happen at the same moment.
- The final sentence is designed to be quoted or forwarded.

### What should not be copied

- Exact identities, wording, character designs, props, and plot turns.
- Generic black-screen series opening if the identity lacks conflict.
- Moralizing without explaining incentives.
- Skipping the business mechanism between poverty and wealth.
- Gender hostility, class contempt, or romantic stereotypes.
- Detailed illegal procedures.
- One-step recovery from addiction or collapse.
- Overwritten metaphors that slow causal understanding.

## Adaptation into commercial-model content

Build each episode from two linked stories:

1. **Human story**: what the protagonist wants, fears, signals, or cannot stop doing.
2. **Commercial story**: which business designs, distributes, finances, or monetizes that behavior.

Recommended bridge:

> You thought you were buying `[product/status/hope]`. The business was actually buying `[your attention, habit, data, future cash flow, referrals, or risk-bearing capacity]`.

End with three layers:

- emotional truth: what the person lost or learned;
- money truth: who earned and who carried the risk;
- action truth: one test, limit, question, or calculation the viewer can use.
