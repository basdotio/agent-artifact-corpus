## Sample 8: transition grammar

The useful pattern is not constant motion. Most individual shots remain visually stable. Energy comes from short, meaningful transitions at scene boundaries.

### Observed transition types

| Type | Approximate duration | Best use | Observed pattern |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Short cross-dissolve | 0.20-0.45s | time, place, or emotional continuity | office to home, account problem to family consequence |
| Double-exposure dissolve | 0.25-0.50s | visible security becoming hidden danger | estate or money image fading into anxiety or collapse |
| Object/idea match cut | 0-0.30s | cause-and-effect explanation | cash to protection structure, digital interface to contract, trust box to beneficiary |
| Hard cut | immediate | numbered method, irreversible turn, legal or causal reveal | abstract claim to concrete document or new chapter |
| Metaphor overlay | 0.20-0.50s | one major risk escalation | office or contract image briefly becoming fire, lock, wall, or crowd |

### General rule

Choose a transition because the relationship between two scenes requires it:

- same action, object, or causal chain: use a match cut;
- passage of time or location: use a short dissolve;
- attractive surface becoming hidden cost: use a double exposure;
- irreversible decision, deadline, or mechanism reveal: use a hard cut;
- emotional aftermath: allow a slightly slower dissolve.

Start the next stable composition as soon as the transition ends. Do not extend the transition into horizontal drift, shaking, or continuous camera wandering.

### What not to learn

- Do not place a fancy transition between every two images.
- Do not use a transition that has no semantic relationship to the narration.
- Do not revive PPT-style panels, English dashboards, or document slides merely because the source uses them.
- Do not confuse cross-dissolve continuity with a requirement to keep every shot moving.

## Sample 9: opening montage

The first five seconds form a compressed trailer for the whole episode.

### Observed sequence

| Time | Visual function | Retention effect |
|---|---|---|
| 0.0-0.4s | narrow horizontal slices reveal luxury, consumption, and distress in fragments | incomplete images create immediate visual questions |
| 0.5-1.4s | rapid flashes of social reward, debt anxiety, collapse, and numbers | previews both desire and final cost before explanation |
| 1.5-2.9s | aspirational beauty, mirror, dessert, and polished lifestyle images | establishes why the temptation feels attractive |
| 3.0-3.6s | brief RGB split/glitch while the consequence identity appears | marks the story's danger label |
| 3.7-5.0s | stabilize on the dorm-room contrast and show the provocative identity | converts montage energy into a readable story world |

### Adaptable opening formula

Use a consequence montage only when every flash will return later:

1. Show 3-6 flashes of desire, decision, reward, warning, and consequence.
2. Make the desire and the damage visually contradictory.
3. Put a readable identity plus concrete cost on screen by about three seconds, even if narration finishes the sentence slightly later.
4. Synchronize major flashes with restrained impacts, whooshes, or music hits.
5. Stabilize into the main animated scene by roughly three to five seconds.
6. After stabilization, return to the normal rule: static shots or restrained center zoom only.

Do not use a generic highlight reel. A flash that never returns is decoration rather than foreshadowing.

## Sample 9: narrative pressure curve

The episode does not descend in one straight line. It alternates reward, pressure, temporary relief, and a worse baseline.

| Approx. time | Beat | Emotional direction | New question |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0:00-0:36 | provocative identity and dorm-room status contrast | discomfort rises | why is she living between two social worlds? |
| 0:36-1:05 | limited budget, shame, and comparison | pressure rises | how will she close the status gap? |
| 1:05-1:50 | “small daily payment” offer and instant credit | hope rises | can one cheap installment change her identity? |
| 1:50-2:40 | new phone, purchases, attention, and social validation | false victory | how long can the polished life last? |
| 2:40-3:16 | repayment calendar, extra work, and food sacrifice | hidden cost rises | which bill breaks first? |
| 3:16-4:00 | low balance, new loan, old bill cleared, extra cash spent | temporary relief, then reversal | why does being “paid off” create more debt? |
| 4:00-4:58 | missed deadline, penalty, contact threat, and extreme loan terms | irreversible trap | how fast does the debt machine compound? |
| 4:58-5:35 | secret double life and debt-rollover arithmetic | pressure accelerates | when does the visible identity collapse? |
| 5:35-6:14 | health damage and failed resale | physical and financial low | can selling status objects still save her? |
| 6:14-6:59 | exact debt total plus a two-minute deadline | final suspense peak | what did all the debt actually buy? |
| 6:59-7:33 | quiet numbness and callback to empty branded objects | cold aftermath | what remains when the status signal loses meaning? |

### Pressure-wave rule

Use this loop only when the protagonist's choices naturally create it. One convincing relief beat is better than several invented ones, and some stories need none. When a relief beat exists, it should solve the immediate symptom while worsening the underlying mechanism:

`pressure -> shortcut -> reward -> hidden obligation -> deadline -> larger shortcut -> higher pressure`

The new baseline after each relief must be worse than before. A temporary win should create a fresh question, not reset the story.

## Viewer-interest triggers

### Status contrast

Put two lives in one frame: inexpensive storage beside luxury packaging, cafeteria food beside a polished social post, visible success beside an empty balance. The viewer understands the conflict before the explanation.

### Micro-price rationalization

Translate an unaffordable total into a familiar small daily amount. Then later restore the total cost, fees, cash timing, and downside. This reveals how pricing changes perception without teaching viewers to borrow recklessly.

### Exact clocks and numbers

Use exact payment dates, balances, countdowns, and totals when they cause the next decision. Numbers such as “due tomorrow,” “32 left,” or an amount precise to cents feel like evidence inside the fictional world.

### Visible and hidden scoreboards

Track the visible signal rising while the hidden financial state falls:

`status objects / likes / revenue / orders rise`

versus

`cash / health / sleep / trust / optionality fall`

### Double life

Contrast public composure with private panic. The secrecy itself raises stakes because asking for help now threatens identity, family trust, or belonging.

### Reversal sentence

Use one line that changes the meaning of the apparent win:

> You thought you were using the rule. The system was using your future cash flow.

### Object callback

Return to an early object at the collapse: phone, packaging, receipt, mirror, bag, account screen, or contract. Its meaning should reverse from hope to cost.

## Adaptation into commercial-model videos

Keep both stories causally linked:

1. Human story: desire, comparison, shame, relief, secrecy, or fear drives a transaction.
2. Commercial story: a platform, lender, brand, franchise, advertiser, or supplier earns from that repeated behavior.

At the final third, explicitly reveal:

- who was paid first;
- which small-price framing or reward reduced resistance;
- how obligations accumulated;
- which party owned the downside;
- what realistic calculation, limit, test, or early-help action could interrupt the loop.

Do not end commercial education at emotional devastation. Preserve the quiet aftermath if useful, then add the money-flow explanation and one practical safeguard.

## Optional diagnostic checks

Use these only when reviewing a complex draft or diagnosing weak retention. Do not output them automatically and do not require every story to contain false wins, relief beats, or a formal pressure curve.

- Every opening flash is a later callback.
- The opening consequence is understandable by three seconds.
- The montage stabilizes by five seconds.
- Every transition has a written semantic reason.
- Transition effects stay at shot boundaries; post-opening shots do not drift horizontally.
- If the story contains a false win or temporary relief, it follows naturally from the protagonist's decision and changes the next consequence.
- Exact numbers cause decisions rather than decorate the script.
- The commercial mechanism appears before the ending and does not feel appended to an unrelated tragedy.
- The episode ends with a verified, realistic safeguard rather than pure fear or despair.
